History of Birgi

Antiquity

In ancient times there was a city called Hypaipa at the foot of Aipos Hill on Mount Tmolos, 9 km west of present-day Birgi and 4 km northwest of Ödemiş. This city, mentioned by ancient writers, must also have existed in the period of the Kingdom of Lydia. The coins minted there, especially in the Roman period (after 133 BC) when the city flourished, show that it continued to exist throughout antiquity. Later called Datbey, the city is known today as Günlüce. Although it went through a troubled time at the beginning of the 7th century AD, it survived as the seat of a bishopric. In the list of bishoprics drawn up under Andronikos II (1282–1328), the name of Birgi (Pyrgion) appears for the first time as a 'metropolitan see'.

The Greek historian and philosopher Strabo describes this region as follows: “As one descends from Bozdağ towards the plain of the Küçükmenderes, there is the city of Hypaepa.” This small city lies one league (fersah) northwest of Ödemiş. Ancient writers, who often mention it, always add the word small: “Bozdağ, which has a steep and difficult descent, slopes down on two sides. On one side it runs towards Sart (Sardis), and on the other it ends at little Hypaepa.”

Birgi lies among the hills on the southern slopes of Bozdağ (2,150 m), on both banks of the Sarıyar stream that comes down from the mountain, at an altitude of 390 metres. Although there are several ancient cities in the area, Birgi developed as a medieval town. Settlement in the area of the ancient city of Hypaipa is thought to go back to around 3000 BC.

Having been an active settlement since those times, Birgi came successively under Phrygian, Lydian, Persian, Pergamene and Roman rule; the development that forms the historical background of today's town took place in the Byzantine period. In this period the town, known as “Pyrgion” or “Pyrgi” and forming the historical basis of Birgi as a settlement, was built, as was typical of the age, as a “castle town” on a relatively high site at the foot of the Bozdağ mountains, overlooking the plain where present-day Ödemiş lies and which seemed safer. The name Pyrgion comes from the Hellenic word pyrgos (tower, bastion) and means a place of towers, a fortress. The town's present name also derives from it.

The history of the region goes back to antiquity. Near Birgi, where Bozdağ lies today, there was a small settlement called Dios Hieron (the place of the temple of Zeus) in antiquity. This settlement also minted coins. The Dios Hieron coins in the British Museum bear a relief of Zeus seated on a throne inside a temple. This god must be the old Anatolian chief god (Tarkhun, Sanda, Adra), given the guise of “Zeus of Tmolos” in the Hellenistic age.

No ancient building can be seen in the town; however, the worked white marble blocks of very grand ancient buildings were reused in large numbers in medieval buildings, especially in the construction of the tomb of Aydınoğlu Mehmet Bey.

Anatolian Seljuk Period

Unlike many other ancient cities, Birgi evidently kept its importance in later periods, and it is said to have been the seat of a bishopric in the Byzantine period. Although the scarcity of studies on this period makes a general assessment difficult, Birgi Castle, which stood in a strategically and geographically important position, is thought to have been built by John II Komnenos (1118–1143) or his successor Manuel Komnenos (1143–1180). Birgi became a metropolitan see at the end of the 12th century, and from this period on it also became the target of frequent Turkish raids.

Towards the end of the 13th century, Birgi and its surroundings are known to have been the target of raids by Sasa Bey, the son-in-law of Menteşe Bey. After the withdrawal of the Catalans, who were active in the region on behalf of the Byzantines, Sasa Bey took the town from the Byzantines in 1304. Sasa Bey's name lives on in a neighbourhood just outside the settlement. The 15th-century Ottoman tax registers (tahrir defterleri) also mention the existence of the Sasalı neighbourhood.

Aydınid Principality Period

After Sasa Bey fell from favour, Aydınoğlu Mehmet Bey became the sole ruler of the area in 1307 and made Birgi the capital of the principality (beylik) he founded. In 1312 he had a mosque, a tomb and a madrasa built here, and the region under his rule came to be known as Memleket-i Birgi (the Land of Birgi). In 1333 the famous Arab traveller Ibn Battuta was a guest of Mehmet Bey for two weeks at his summer residence on Bozdağ and in his palace in Birgi. Battuta, who describes Birgi as a beautiful and rich town, also writes in detail about Mehmet Bey's character and about the palace of Birgi.

When Mehmet Bey died in 1334, he was buried in the monumental tomb right next to the mosque he had built. He was succeeded by his eldest son, Bahaüddin Umur Bey (1334–1348). During this time Umur Bey took part in many naval battles and, together with his men, led numerous expeditions along the Aegean coasts. Although Umur Bey chose Birgi as his seat of government, because of his naval activities he generally preferred to stay in İzmir.

After Umur Bey died in 1348 during a campaign against the Latins holding the harbour castle of İzmir, he was succeeded by his brother Hızır Bey. The articles of a treaty made with the Latins at this time give various details about the economic structure of the principality.

The region, which passed to Fahreddin İsa Bey in the 1360s, was handed over to the Ottomans by İsa Bey around 1391. After more than ten years under Ottoman rule, Birgi came under Aydınid control again after the Battle of Ankara, and in 1425 it was finally taken under Ottoman rule. After this period the settlement, now the centre of a district (kaza) of the Sanjak of Aydın, became one of the largest towns of Western Anatolia between 1425 and 1575. In the mid-15th century Birgi was a town of nine neighbourhoods with a population of nearly 1,200.

Ottoman Period

Under Ottoman rule Birgi, the centre of a district (kaza) of the Sanjak of Aydın, showed slow and steady growth, especially from the 15th century until the 19th century. In 1451 it was a town of nine neighbourhoods with a population of 1,200.

In the 16th century a significant increase in the number of neighbourhoods in Birgi stands out. According to foundation (vakıf) records, buildings in Birgi at that time included the Aydınoğlu Mehmed Bey Mosque (Ulu Cami) and its madrasa, the Aydınoğlu İsa Bey Mosque and Tomb, the Hayreddin Masjid, the Kurt Kadı Masjid, the Seferşah Masjid, the Ahi Hayreddin Zawiya, the Silahtar Ahmed Zawiya, the Hacı Ahmed Zawiya and the Şeyh Muhuddin Zawiya.

Mehmed Efendi, a famous scholar of the time, was appointed as professor (müderris) to Birgi, which was an important cultural centre for many years; he continued his scholarly life in this town and became famous under the name Birgivi.

At the beginning of the 17th century Birgi was affected not only by economic hardship but also by the unrest in the region. Cennetoğlu of Birgi, a former timariot cavalryman (tımarlı sipahi), shut himself up in the castle and launched attacks, took control of the Aydın area and captured Tire and Turgutlu; however, he was defeated by the Ottoman army on the Manisa plain, brought back to Birgi and executed. Birgi is thought to have been damaged during these struggles. According to an avarız tax survey of this period, the Hisariçi (inner castle) neighbourhood disappeared after the rebellion and other neighbourhoods were formed in its place.

In the mid-17th century the wealthy merchant Derviş Ağa is known to have built a Dar al-Hadith madrasa and then a mosque in Birgi, and Hacı Arif Mehmet Efendi a library. In the same period the town went through two major earthquakes.

Evliya Çelebi, who came to the town around this time, writes that Birgi had a castle at the foot of Bozdağ whose outer walls were in ruins, with four hundred houses inside the castle; in the lower town there were eighteen neighbourhoods, twenty-four mosques and masjids, about two thousand six hundred houses, seven primary schools (sıbyan mektebi), two hundred shops, two inns (han) and a covered market (bedesten), and the people's main livelihood was the trade in white thread. Evliya Çelebi also describes the Ulu Cami and the Derviş Ağa Mosque in detail.

By the 18th century an economic and cultural revival in the region stands out. One of the largest foundations of this period was the complex (külliye) built by Karazade Hacı Mustafa Efendi, a wealthy man of Birgi, and completed in 1782; it consisted of a large inn, a domed mosque, a madrasa and a primary school. Of these buildings, only the mosque has survived. The many water mills, olive-oil works, rice mills, workplaces, weavers, tailors and shoemakers mentioned in the foundation records are clear signs of Birgi's wealth in this period. Grand, richly decorated houses, some of which, such as the Çakırağa Mansion, have survived to the present day, were built in this period.

In the 19th century Birgi began to shrink as Ödemiş and other settlements on the plain grew. Various documents show that the town's population fell sharply around the middle of the century. The French traveller Texier, who came to the region at the beginning of the 19th century, writes with interest that Birgi was built over the bed of a torrent rushing down from Bozdağ, that an elegant bridge joined the two sides of the town, and about its beautiful trees and colourful houses. Texier also mentions that the great mosque was covered with a lead dome, and describes its arches and capitals made from ancient columns.

Republican Period and Today

The beginning of the 20th century was a very difficult time for Birgi, as for many settlements in the region. Floods and fires caused serious damage to the town. In 1919 the Greek army, advancing from İzmir, occupied the surrounding towns, including Birgi. In 1922, after the Great Offensive, Greek irregulars set fire to the town and more than half of Birgi burned down. The town was rebuilt as a result of planning work carried out in the early years of the Republic. During the reconstruction, some of the historic buildings that were derelict and out of use were also demolished. The madrasa of Karaoğlu Mustafa Efendi was demolished in 1935, and the Hacı Osman Madrasa and the Aydınoğlu Mehmet Bey Madrasa in 1944; an earthquake in the same year damaged the Ulu Cami and some other buildings. From the 1960s onwards the condition of the town began to improve, and the attention it receives has grown steadily.

Because of its rich and varied historic fabric, the whole of Birgi was declared a conservation area (SİT) in 1996. According to the Ministry of Culture and Tourism's “Birgi Cultural Inventory”, there are 11 monumental buildings (hamams, madrasas and mosques), 110 natural assets, examples of civil architecture and registered cultural assets, registered in different years. Birgi is one of the settlements on the UNESCO World Heritage Tentative List. In 2022 it was selected by the World Tourism Organization as one of the 32 best tourism villages in the world.
