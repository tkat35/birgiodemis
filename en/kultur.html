Food

Kulak çorbası, sini pidesi, tombaç kebabı, peksimet avukması, sarmaşık kavurması, kabak çiçeği dolması, kabak çiçeği kızartması, keşkek, kestirme çorbası, sura, kuzu kapama, gumbar dolması, yağlı sulu akıtma, yörük katmeri, ekmek dolması, höşmerim, heybeli çorba, ıspanak oturtması, pirinçli ıspanak, kalburabastı tatlısı, cevizli yufka tatlısı, lokma tatlısı, sütlü kabak tatlısı, kabak hoşafı, vişneli ekmek tatlısı, köpük helvası, incir ısması.

Although you may not find all these local dishes of Birgi in restaurants, they are dishes that the people of the area have been cooking and eating at home for many years.

Even so, the cafés and restaurants of Birgi offer many delicious dishes to suit different tastes. The local spread breakfasts (serpme kahvaltı) served in boutique places are also very rich. While you are here, do not leave without tasting the famous Ödemiş köfte (kebab) and Töngül pide, a speciality of Ödemiş, and sipping your coffee in Birgi's square. Enjoy your meal!

Customs and Traditions

Folk Dances

Men's dances: Harmandalı zeybeği, Kocaarap zeybeği, İkiparmak zeybeği, the knife dance (Kasap havası), Bozdağ zeybeği, Köroğlu, Aydın zeybeği, Çiftetelli.

Women's dances: Meşeli, Sepetçioğlu, Çiftetelli, Uzun uzun kavaklar, Entarisi albasma, Mavi çember oyası, Geline bak geline, Entarisi lacivert, Kayısıdır kayısı, Al entarim yünmedik, Pembeleri giymişsin, Bahçelerde börülce, Ördek suya dal da gel, Ak üzüm, Kız saçların saçların, Pembe giyerim pembe, Sarı kurdelem sarı, Tin tin tinimini hanım, Dol kara bakır dol.

Local Clothing

In earlier times women wore the üçetek (a three-skirted robe), which could be embroidered in bindallı style. Everyday clothes were şalvar (baggy trousers) below and a collarless shirt (işlik) above. In the cold of winter a jacket called “Libade” (or perhaps Lüberde) was worn over these. Unmarried girls and young married women tied a showy headscarf (çember) edged with needle lace, and older women a plain one, also edged with needle lace; over this they wore a değirme cloth embroidered in counted-thread work (hesap işi). New brides, if they wished, wore a “grep” edged with needle lace. Outdoor clothing was a silk peştemal and a “pembezar bürgü” worn over the head. Earlier still, a peştemal of red Buldan cloth was worn. The silk çanlı zar worn by some women was black in winter and white in summer. Today modern clothes such as suits, coats, and skirts and blouses are worn. Men's clothing used to be like zeybek costume (made up of the cepken, camadan, çakşır, menevrek and kepmen). Some men wore the “dimidon”. After 1923 riding breeches, shirts and jackets, with a felt hat (fötr) or a cap, became fashionable. A silk yağlık or uladı was used as a handkerchief.

Handicrafts

Needle lace (iğne oyası), crochet lace (tığ oyası), shuttle lace (mekik oyası), cross-stitch (kanaviçe), counted-thread embroidery (hesap işi), metal-strip embroidery (tel kırma), wrapped embroidery (sarma), bead lace (boncuk oyası), rag weaving (çaputçul), sheet weaving, silk pembezar weaving, thread weaving and yağlık (handkerchief) weaving are practised.

Houses and Daily Life

The houses of Birgi, which has largely preserved its historic architectural fabric, are mostly two-storey houses of the “Hanay” type, with a “Çardak” (gallery) facing the inner courtyard in front of the rooms built side by side. The doors of the rooms and the gallery face the inner courtyard to protect the family's privacy. The ground floor is mostly a stable where animals (such as horses, donkeys, cows and sheep) are kept and where some farming tools are stored. Some farmers keep agricultural produce such as olive oil in large jars or drums on the ground floor. Things like bales of straw, seed potatoes and sacks of barley may also be stored there. This floor has no windows. In two-storey Hanay houses, the street-facing windows that light the rooms are mostly on the upper floor. Some houses have bay windows (cumba) projecting over the street. From the windows of the bay, both ends of the street can be watched more easily. In some houses wooden lattices were fitted to the windows, both in the bays and in the walls, to protect the family's privacy. In single-storey houses too, the gallery faces the inner courtyard. Because of the unpleasant smell, the toilet is outside the house, in a corner of the garden. In mansion-type buildings, such as the Çakırağa Mansion, the toilet is inside. The bathroom is a narrow closed cupboard called the “Gusulhane”, a continuation of the built-in cupboard called the “Yüklük” in the bedroom. In the rooms, cushions are laid on the low built-in benches (“Seki”) fixed to the floor along the walls. For comfortable sitting, the backrests are padded with “tepme” cushions. One edge of the covers spread over these cushions is decorated by young girls with knitted lace called “maklet” or with hand embroidery.

Kilims, felt or carpets are spread on the floors of the rooms. In the corridors of very old houses, fruits such as quinces, pomegranates and apples arranged on shelves fixed at about head height fill the house with a lovely smell. Some people also line up bowls and pots on these shelves, which are also called “elmalık başı”. In winter the house is heated by a fire lit in the fireplace, also called the “yer ocağı” (floor hearth). The hearths are also where food is cooked. Today some families heat their homes with charcoal braziers (mangal) and stoves. Some families have also had decorative motifs painted on the interior walls of their houses, which are painted in various colours.

Legends

The King's Daughter

Once upon a time the King of Pyrgion (Birgi) had a dazzling daughter. She was a lively, cheerful girl whose beauty caught everyone's eye. She could never keep still; she was as mischievous and as lively as can be.

Our story takes place in 1307. At that time Aydınoğlu Mehmet Bey, an officer of Yakup bin Alişir, the Bey of Germiyan, besieged the town of Pyrgion (Birgi), ruled by the Byzantine Emperor, with a mounted and armed Turkmen regiment. When the king's daughter saw the handsome Turkmen leader Mehmet Bey on the walls besieging the town, she was deeply moved and fell in love with him. The beautiful girl expressed her feelings to Mehmet Bey in letters, and through these letters they began to write to each other. In time, the king's daughter found inner peace, grew calm, and became a Muslim for the sake of her love. In her last letter, she revealed to Mehmet Bey the most poorly defended gate of Birgi Castle.

Attacking through that gate with his Turkmen army, Mehmet Bey succeeded in entering and taking the town. But the priests and men living in the town realised the girl's betrayal, though too late. Before fleeing by the Kemer Yaylası road, they found the king's daughter and threw her down from the wall of the church. Where her feet struck the marble, the prints of her clogs (takunya) remained, and she lost her life in that cruel fall.

When Mehmet Bey entered the town, he had the body of the king's daughter, who had become a Muslim before her death, found, and she was buried according to Muslim tradition. On her gravestone he had written: “The late, the innocent, the forgiven, the blessed, the martyr.” Even today, in Birgi, you can see this grave and the stone with the clog prints to the right of the entrance of the Aydınid Tomb.

Küp Uçuranlar (Those Who Fly Jars)

Long ago, a sorcerer elder (dede) lived in Birgi. A tower made of black stones was his lodge (dergâh), and he had disciples devoted to him. His miraculous power was to send flying jars to the neighbouring village to do harm, and he and some of his disciples could ride on jars and fly. One day a mullah came to the sorcerer's lodge, and the elder accepted him into his community. Then one day, while the sorcerer was flying on his jar during an attack on the neighbouring village, the newcomer found a secret trapdoor in the lodge. When he opened it, he saw that below was the lodge's cesspit, and that a Qur'an had been hung on a rope between the trapdoor and the cesspit. He took the Qur'an out, and the moment he did so, the sorcerer's spell was broken: the jars and the disciples who had gone to attack the neighbouring village crashed to the ground. After this, the sorcerer's community broke up, and he himself left Birgi in shame. It is also said that some of his disciples were killed by the villagers of the neighbouring village.
